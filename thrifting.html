The price of a T‑shirt

What you pay at the register is nothing compared to what the planet and its people pay.
By Austin Liu · 28 July 2026

To the average person, a cotton t‑shirt and jeans cost nothing more than 25 bucks. But behind the price tag, there is a much bigger human and environmental cost.

So how much do your clothes really cost? The production processes of cotton denim are extremely meticulous, using tons of water, chemical treatments, and energy. Through the whole process of growing cotton, water‑dyeing and cleaning, one kilogram of cotton uses around ten thousand liters of water — and global cotton production alone consumes over 250 billion tons of water annually [1]. Toxic dyes and chemicals used for treatment are disposed of directly into rivers in developing countries like Bangladesh, India and China, and fabric production is responsible for 20% of GHG emissions worldwide.

Polyester and acrylic fabrics deplete non‑renewable fossil fuels, and produce upwards of 1 million microplastics each wash, responsible for 10% of the microplastics around the world. You are literally eating and breathing your clothes.

“The overproduction causes 70% of clothes to be dumped one year after production, and 40% of all clothes are never even worn.”

The sweatshop workers who are forced to make these garments work in extremely unsafe environments, often in unpaid overtime. Of the 60 million factory workers worldwide, less than 2% earn a living wage [1]. The factory owners, who think about nothing but profits, pay the average worker 8 dollars a day. Workers, seen as disposable labor, are squeezed to give maximum output. They suffer under the demands of fast fashion, where companies compete to produce the most amount of clothes at the cheapest price possible in the shortest time to catch the next microtrend.

The overproduction causes 70% of clothes to be dumped one year after production, and 40% of all clothes are never even worn [1]. Unable to turn back into usable fabric, they are piled into chemical‑leaking, poisonous gas‑spewing incineration plants, or landfills next to communities far away from your home. Garment production shows no signs of stopping; in fact, it is only increasing at an exponential rate.

Realize that your clothes might only cost you 25 bucks, but every piece of fast fashion you buy or throw away is irreversible damage to the environment and fellow human beings around the world.

It’s not your fault that nobody has told you this. However, you now have the power to make a positive change.

Take your time to care for the environment, and fast fashion workers, and go thrift shopping! Thrifting is better for your pocket and the environment, because it gives clothes a second life without directly funding the child‑labor‑abusing, worker‑exploiting industry owners [1]. Yet, be mindful — recent research shows that secondhand consumption can sometimes supplement, rather than replace, new purchases [1]. So while thrifting is a great step, it’s most effective when paired with a genuine reduction in overall consumption. Trashing your clothes might be the easiest way to get rid of them, but take your time to donate, or sell them to a thrift store! Do not take more than you need.

On your next clothes‑shopping trip, take a step back and think carefully about your purchase. How long will it last? Will it be worn regularly? Will it look “too old” for the next trend? Are there already enough clothes at home? Could this have been thrifted instead? Does the brand treat workers fairly?

Beyond the price tag: the hidden chain of injustice. The true cost of a $5 T‑shirt begins in cotton fields drenched with pesticides that poison local water tables, and ends in incinerators that release dioxins into the air. Between those two points, workers in low‑income countries are routinely denied breaks, exposed to hazardous fibers, and threatened with dismissal if they organize. Fast‑fashion brands have turned garment production into a race to the bottom, where profit margins are protected by exploiting the most vulnerable. [1] The system is designed to hide these realities behind shiny storefronts and influencer hauls.

The psychology of overconsumption. We buy clothes not just because we need them, but because we are told we need them. Micro‑trends, limited drops, and 24‑hour sales engineer a sense of urgency. And when we donate or resell, we often feel virtuous — but that goodwill can mask the fact that we are still consuming at a rate that far exceeds what the planet can bear. [1] The most sustainable garment is the one already in your closet.

What about “green” collections? Many brands promote eco‑lines made from recycled polyester or organic cotton. But these collections often represent less than 1% of total production, while the rest continues business as usual. Meanwhile, the term “circular” is used loosely — true circularity requires closed‑loop systems that keep materials in use indefinitely, not just a second cycle. Until production volumes drop and wages rise, these initiatives remain greenwashing. [1]

Your choices matter, but so does collective action. Individual shifts — thrifting, repairing, swapping — are powerful and necessary. But lasting change also demands policy: extended producer responsibility, truth‑in‑labeling for sustainability claims, and living wage mandates. Support brands that publish transparent supply chains, and vote for leaders who prioritize climate and labor justice. The fashion system won't change overnight, but every mindful purchase sends a message that exploitation is no longer in style.

✶  Before you click “add to cart” ✶

  • Will I wear this at least 30 times? (The 30‑wear rule)
  • Can I mend it if it tears? (Repair is resistance)
  • Am I buying because I'm bored, anxious, or scrolling?
  • Could I borrow, swap, or thrift something similar?
  • Does the brand publish its factory list and sustainability reports?

The industry will tell you that sustainable fashion is expensive or inconvenient. But the true inconvenience is living on a planet with poisoned rivers, burning landfills, and exploited children. Your 25‑dollar t‑shirt is a bargain only if you ignore the bill that comes due for all of us.
